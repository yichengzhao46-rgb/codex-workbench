# M019

## 1. Figure identity

- Legacy ID: U005
- Journal: Angewandte Chemie International Edition (2026)
- Figure: Figure 6g working-model panel
- Role in PR20: membrane-resolved EET / respiratory electron-routing reference

## 2. Functional role

This figure explains a proposed noncanonical respiratory dehalogenation and electron-transfer network at membrane/periplasm scale. Its main communication task is **routing electrons through compartments and candidate molecular conduits**.

## 3. Layout and reading logic

The figure uses a horizontal membrane cutaway with three clearly separated zones: external/periplasmic region above, inner membrane across the lower third, and cytosol below. The FeS bridge and membrane protein modules create a left-to-right and bottom-to-top electron path.

Reading order is driven by the electron arrows rather than panel boundaries. The viewer follows electron entry, quinone cycling, FeS/cytochrome transfer, redox-protein/flavin exchange, reductive dehalogenase access and ATP synthesis.

## 4. Cell / object rendering

No whole-cell morphology is shown. Instead, the visual unit is the membrane compartment. Bilayers are drawn as repeated phospholipid motifs; proteins are simplified colored blocks/capsules. The potential FeS bridge is represented as a chain of dark spheres, giving it a unique visual identity distinct from proteins.

This is a strong example of choosing the **right biological scale** for the question rather than forcing a full cell into the figure.

## 5. Arrow grammar

Electron flow is the dominant grammar. Red and blue arrows plus explicit `e−` labels identify transfer direction. Black curved arrows handle quinone cycling and coupled reactions. Dashed arrows mark proposed or less-certain links.

This separation between solid established-looking routes and dashed candidate routes is especially valuable for evidence-boundary control.

## 6. Palette / outline / typography

The membrane uses muted lilac/purple, keeping it visually subordinate. Proteins use soft pastel fills with dark outlines. Electron arrows use saturated accents, so the pathway is more visually prominent than the components themselves.

Typography is dense and technical. Compartment labels and protein-family names do most of the explanatory work.

## 7. Information density and evidence style

Density is high. Many proteins, cofactors and candidate routes coexist in one panel. The design is mechanism-first rather than data-first: experimental support is assumed to exist elsewhere in the figure set.

## 8. Best reusable elements

- membrane/periplasm/cytosol compartment scaffold
- conductive bridge as a distinct repeated-particle motif
- explicit quinone cycle loop
- strong electron-arrow color semantics
- dashed candidate-pathway notation
- protein modules as simple functional blocks
- ATP synthase placed as a terminal energy-conservation module

## 9. Suitable future use cases in the Bath–RP project

Best suited to:

- Bath outward EET mechanism summaries;
- potential EEU reverse-routing diagrams;
- Bath–Bio-Se membrane-interface hypotheses;
- GAC/material-facilitated electron-transfer figures where a membrane-level route must be distinguished from the external material pathway;
- diagrams that need to separate established cytochrome/quinone modules from candidate conduits.

## 10. Weaknesses / what not to imitate

The figure is very label-dense and assumes expert familiarity. Do not transfer every protein family into a Bath figure unless the evidence directly supports it. For presentation use, simplify to the few modules required by the hypothesis and preserve the compartment/arrow grammar rather than the full molecular inventory.
